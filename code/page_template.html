Did the pits closing still show?

At the start of 1984 Britain still had a deep coal industry. Within ten years almost all of it had gone. I mapped every deep mine that was working that year, found the towns that grew up around them, and compared those towns in the 2021 Census with towns of the same size, in the same regions, that never had a pit.

The great miners' strike began on {{context.strike_start|raw}}, with a walkout at Cortonwood Colliery in South Yorkshire, and ended a year later, on {{context.strike_end|raw}}. Wikipedia's article on the strike says {{context.pits_1984_wiki|int}} pits were still working in 1984. The last deep mine in Britain, Kellingley in North Yorkshire, closed on {{context.kellingley_close|raw}}.

The strike is still argued over, and this page does not take sides on it. My question is a narrower one, and it can be put to data. Forty years on, do the places that lived by the pits still look different from other places? And if they do, does it matter whether their pit closed in the first wave after the strike or held on into this century?

The answer is a clear yes to the first question and, more surprisingly, mostly no to the second. But there is an important catch: the coalfields were different from other places long before 1984, and my data cannot see that far back.

01 · The pitsEvery deep mine, year by year

There is no single open list of the pits working in 1984, so I built one. For Yorkshire, Wikipedia's List of collieries in Yorkshire (1984 to 2015) gives every pit and its closure year. For the rest of England, Wales and Scotland I used the Northern Mine Research Society's tables of the National Coal Board's collieries, keeping every colliery that closed in 1984 or later, and checked the years against Wikipedia's lists for Derbyshire and the United Kingdom. Where both give a year, they agree exactly for {{pits.share_year_exact|pct}} of {{pits.n_year_checked|int}} pits and within a year for {{pits.share_year_within1|pct}}; where they differ I use Wikipedia's year, since anyone can check it.

That gives {{pits.n|int}} colliery units: {{pits.by_country.England|int}} in England, {{pits.by_country.Wales|int}} in Wales and {{pits.by_country.Scotland|int}} in Scotland. It runs a little above the {{context.pits_1984_wiki|int}} usually quoted, because some combined mines appear under more than one name. I placed {{pits.placing.osm_colliery|int}} pits at a colliery mapped in OpenStreetMap, {{pits.placing.wikidata|int}} at their Wikidata entry and the other {{pits.placing.village|int}} at the village or town the pit was named for.

The closures came fast. The worst single year was {{pits.peak_year|raw}}, the year the strike ended, when {{pits.peak_n|int}} of the pits closed; {{pits.n_1985_86|int}} went in 1985 and 1986 together. A second wave followed from 1988 to 1994, including the programme announced in October 1992, which brought {{pits.n_1992_94|int}} closures in 1992 to 1994. By the end of 1994, when the industry was privatised, {{pits.n_by_1994|int}} of the {{pits.n|int}} pits had gone ({{pits.share_by_1994|pct}}). The remaining {{pits.n_1995_2015|int}} closed one by one over the next twenty years, the last three in 2015: Hatfield, Thoresby and Kellingley.

02 · The townsWhich towns were pit towns?

To turn pits into towns I used the ONS Built-up Areas of 2022, which cover every town and village in Great Britain. A pit belongs to a town if it lies within 3 km of the town's centre, or within 2 km of its edge for larger towns (I treat each town as a circle with the same area). Pits often stood just outside the village they fed, so the rule is generous. Then I sorted every town into four groups:

  • Pit towns: at least one of the 1984 pits within reach.
  • Coalfield neighbours: no pit within reach, but one within 10 km of the town's edge. Many of their people will have worked at the pits too, so I keep them apart.
  • Older mining towns: no 1984 pit near, but a colliery named in OpenStreetMap or Wikidata within reach. Most of these pits closed long before 1984.
  • Comparison towns: none of the above.

For the comparisons I keep to the {{towns.n_sample|int}} built-up areas in England and Wales with 2,000 to 100,000 people in 2021, because Census 2021 figures for Scotland are not in my data. Among them are {{towns.groups_sample.pit|int}} pit towns, home to {{towns.pop_pit_sample|int}} people, {{towns.groups_sample.near|int}} coalfield neighbours, {{towns.groups_sample.older|int}} older mining towns and {{towns.groups_sample.none|int}} comparison towns.

03 · TodayHow do the towns compare?

For each town I took eight figures from Census 2021 and the ONS income estimates: the share of adults long-term sick or disabled; the share economically inactive for reasons other than retirement or study; the share in work; the shares in routine or semi-routine jobs and in managerial or professional jobs; the share of residents aged 65 or over; the share of households without a car or van; and mean household income (for the financial year ending 2023, available for {{headline.income_n|int}} of the towns).

Size for size, and region for region, the pit towns have {{headline.sick_pts|ppabs}} percentage points more adults who are long-term sick or disabled (95% interval {{headline.sick_lo|ppabs}} to {{headline.sick_hi|ppabs}}). Against a comparison town median of {{outcomes.median.none.pct_sick|pct1}}, that is about {{headline.sick_ratio|f1}} times as many. They have {{headline.inact_pts|ppabs}} points more adults out of work for reasons other than retirement or study, {{headline.routine_pts|ppabs}} points more in routine or semi-routine jobs, {{headline.mgr_pts|ppabs}} points fewer in managerial or professional jobs, {{headline.nocar_pts|ppabs}} points more households without a car, and about {{headline.income_gbp|gbpabs}} a year less household income ({{headline.income_pct_of_none|pctabs}} of the comparison towns' median).

Forty years on, the pit towns are poorer and sicker than similar towns that never had a pit. The gap is not small, and it holds in England and in Wales alike.

Two results are less obvious. The pit towns are younger, with {{headline.age65_pts|ppabs}} points fewer residents aged 65 or over. And their share of adults in work is no different from the comparison towns ({{headline.work_pts|pp}} points, well within chance). That is because fewer of their adults are retired: among those of working age, more are out of work through sickness or disability. Allowing for the share aged 65 or over leaves the sickness gap at {{checks.age_control.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points.

The older mining towns, whose pits mostly closed long before 1984, show the same pattern at roughly half to two thirds the size: {{headline.sick_older_pts|ppabs}} points more long-term sick, {{headline.routine_older_pts|ppabs}} points more routine jobs and {{headline.income_older_gbp|gbpabs}} less income. The coalfield neighbours sit closer again to the comparison towns ({{headline.sick_near_pts|ppabs}} points more long-term sick, {{headline.income_near_gbp|gbpabs}} less income). The nearer a town was to the 1984 pits, the bigger the gap.

04 · TimingDid it matter when the pit closed?

If the closures themselves left the scar, you might expect towns whose pit shut in 1985 to look worse than towns whose pit worked on into the 2000s. I split the pit towns by the year their last pit closed: {{timing.n.early|int}} in 1984 to 1987, {{timing.n.mid|int}} in 1988 to 1994 and {{timing.n.late|int}} in 1995 to 2015.

Pit towns by when their last pit closed
Median share of adults long-term sick or disabled, and inactive for other reasons than retirement or study, 2021. Comparison towns for reference.

There is no clear difference. Compared with towns of the same size and region whose last pit closed in 1984 to 1987, those whose pit lasted until 1995 or later have {{headline.late_sick_pts|ppabs}} points {{headline.late_sick_word|raw}} long-term sickness, well within chance, and no clear difference in income either. Each extra decade the pit stayed open goes with a difference in sickness of {{headline.decade_sick_pts|f2}} points, which is negligible. The towns that lost their pit in the 2010s look much like those that lost it in the 1980s.

I can think of two readings, and the data cannot choose between them. One is that the gap is about the coalfields as places, not about the date of a closure: the industrial structure, the health legacy of mining work, the kind of jobs that came after. The other is that by the time the late survivors closed they employed far fewer local men than in 1984, so their towns had already been through most of the change. Either way, the date on the closure notice is not what separates these towns now.

05 · What this cannot showThe coalfields before 1984, and other limits

Association, not cause. The coalfields were not like other places before the closures. Mining towns were built for the pits, their work was manual, and mining itself left men with damaged lungs and backs. I have no town figures from 1981 or 1991 in my data, so I cannot compare before and after, and some of today's gap was surely there in 1984. The older mining towns, whose pits closed decades earlier and which show a similar but smaller gap, fit that view: the coalfields have a long shadow, and 1984 to 1994 is only its latest part.

Which towns count. My rule, a pit within 3 km of the centre or 2 km of the edge, is rough. With a tighter rule (2 km, or 1 km beyond the edge) {{checks.tight.n_pit|int}} towns count as pit towns and the sickness gap is {{checks.tight.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points; with a looser one (5 km, or 3 km beyond the edge) {{checks.wide.n_pit|int}} towns and {{checks.wide.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points. Keeping only towns of 5,000 or more gives {{checks.over5k.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points, only towns at least 15 km from a city of 100,000 gives {{checks.away_from_cities.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}}, and only the coalfield regions gives {{checks.coal_regions.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}}. In England alone the gap is {{checks.england.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points and in Wales {{checks.wales.models.pct_sick.size_region_pit.b|ptsabs}} points.

The comparison towns are not untouched. Many lost steelworks, mills or factories in the same years. My comparison asks how pit towns differ from other towns of their size and region, not from places that escaped deindustrialisation.

How I did this

Pits. Every colliery in the Northern Mine Research Society's tables of National Coal Board collieries in England, Wales and Scotland that closed in 1984 or later, with Wikipedia's List of collieries in Yorkshire (1984 to 2015) for Yorkshire ({{pits.n_york_wiki|int}} rows) and its Derbyshire and United Kingdom lists as checks; Wikipedia's year is used where both give one. Each pit is placed at an OpenStreetMap feature whose name is the pit's name followed by Colliery, Pit, Mine or Drift within 12 km of its village ({{pits.placing.osm_colliery|int}}), else at a Wikidata mine with a matching label ({{pits.placing.wikidata|int}}), else at its village in OS Open Names ({{pits.placing.village|int}}).

Towns and outcomes. ONS Built-up Areas 2022 for Great Britain; Census 2021 tables TS001, TS007A, TS045, TS062 and TS066 summed from LSOAs by the ONS best-fit lookup; mean household income from the ONS small area estimates (FYE2023), population-weighted over best-fit MSOAs. Older collieries: OpenStreetMap features and Wikidata items named as collieries or coal pits more than 1.5 km from a 1984 pit.

Comparisons. Least-squares regressions of each outcome on the town group, log population and its square, and region, with robust standard errors; checks add the log distance to the nearest built-up area of 100,000 or more, the share aged 65 or over, tighter and looser town rules, and subsets. A nearest-neighbour match within region on population gives a sickness gap of {{outcomes.matched_pit.pct_sick.diff|ptsabs}} points and an income gap of {{outcomes.matched_pit.income_fye2023.diff|gbpabs}}. These are associations, not causes.
